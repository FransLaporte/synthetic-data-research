\section{Synthetic data generation approaches}
\label{sec:rq1}

Generation methods differ in whether they fit relationships from examples
or explicitly describe scenes and their observation. This review organises
them into statistical models, simulation, learned generators and hybrids.
These overlapping groups help compare control and data dependence
\parencite{hernandezSyntheticDataGeneration2022,nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019}.
Related \gls{augmentation} methods transform or recombine existing examples,
with usefulness depending on whether the required labels remain valid
\parencite{nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019}.

\subsection{Statistical models and simulation}

Statistical generators sample fitted distributions, for example using
\glspl{bayesian-network} to represent conditional dependencies.
Hernandez et al. and Hansen et al. discuss such methods for tabular data
\parencite{hernandezSyntheticDataGeneration2022,hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023}.
Their relevance here is the principle that generated samples reflect the
relationships captured by a model, rather than direct evidence of LiDAR
performance. More samples do not automatically resolve uncertainty in the
fitted generator \parencite{breugelSyntheticDataReal2023}.

Simulation instead specifies scene content and how a sensor observes it.
Procedural rules provide control over objects and configurations, while
observation models convert geometry into measurements. This supports
annotation generation but retains limitations from available assets and
omitted sensor effects. \Gls{domain-randomization} broadens selected
conditions, although its usefulness depends on which parameters are varied
and how they are sampled \parencite{nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019}.
Scene variation therefore cannot by itself correct an inadequate sensor model.

\subsection{Learned generators and hybrids}

Learned approaches include \glspl{vae}, which use latent representations,
\glspl{gan}, which use adversarial training, and \glspl{diffusion-model},
which learn denoising processes. Hansen et al. compare representatives in
a tabular setting, without establishing a preferred family for LiDAR
\parencite{hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023}.
Their dependence on examples makes coverage important: plausible samples
can coexist with missing variation or \gls{mode-collapse}
\parencite{breugelSyntheticDataReal2023}.

Hybrids combine explicit structure with learned components. LiDARsim, for
example, couples reconstructed geometry and \gls{ray-casting} with learned
\gls{ray-drop} \parencite{manivasagamLiDARsimRealisticLiDAR2020}.
Such combinations offer geometric control while retaining dependence on
real recordings. Across these approaches, control, coverage and annotation
availability are therefore more informative than dataset size alone.
The next section examines these trade-offs in concrete LiDAR pipelines.
