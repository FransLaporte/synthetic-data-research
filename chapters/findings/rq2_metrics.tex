\section{Evaluation of Synthetic LiDAR Data}
\label{sec:lidar-evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation of neural networks}
\label{sec:lidar-eval-networks}

To evaluate the performance of an image processing network, in general a
separate validation dataset is used. This separation is important because
performance on the training or validation data does not necessarily
represent performance on unseen data~\parencite{raschkaModelEvaluationModel2020}.
In particular, repeatedly using the test set during model development can
result in overly optimistic estimates of performance~\parencite{westphalImprovingModelSelection2019}.

For supervised perception models, the evaluation metric depends on the task.
Classification is commonly evaluated using parameters such as accuracy,
precision, recall, or F1-score (combination of precision and recall), whereas
object detection is commonly evaluated using average precision (AP) or mean
average precision (mAP). For segmentation, Intersection over Union (IoU),
often averaged over classes as mean IoU (mIoU), is commonly used. The
evaluation is performed against annotations in data that are independent of
the data used for model training.

The validation dataset not being used for training is especially important
when evaluating a model trained on synthetic data. A model achieving high
performance on a synthetic validation set only demonstrates that it can
perform well within the synthetic domain. It does not demonstrate that the
model will generalise to real-world data. Therefore, when the purpose of
synthetic data is to train a perception model for autonomous driving,
evaluation on an independent real-world dataset provides stronger evidence of
its utility.

\subsection{Evaluation of synthetic LiDAR datasets}
\label{sec:lidar-eval-datasets}

Synthetic LiDAR datasets can be evaluated at several levels. An important
approach is to train perception models using synthetic point clouds and
subsequently evaluate these models on real-world LiDAR data. This establishes
whether the synthetic dataset contains information that transfers from the
simulated domain to the target real-world domain.

LiDARsim~\parencite{manivasagamLiDARsimRealisticLiDAR2020} gives an example of this approach. The simulator is evaluated by
training perception models using simulated LiDAR and testing them on
real-world datasets. For segmentation, models trained with LiDARsim data are
evaluated on SemanticKITTI, while for vehicle detection, simulated data are
used to train a detector that is evaluated on KITTI. The authors compare
LiDARsim against both real data and the CARLA simulator. Their experiments
therefore evaluate the simulator not only according to the appearance of
individual point clouds, but according to the downstream performance obtained
when synthetic LiDAR is used as training data.

This type of evaluation can be extended by combining synthetic and real data.
The resulting performance can be compared with a real-only baseline. Such an
experiment answers a different question from synthetic-only training: rather
than asking whether synthetic data can replace real data, it investigates
whether synthetic data provides additional information that improves the
perception model. SynLiDAR~\parencite{xiaoTransferLearningSynthetic2022}, for example, investigates synthetic-to-real transfer
for semantic segmentation. Their experiments include synthetic data
augmentation, semi-supervised domain adaptation, and unsupervised domain
adaptation. Adding SynLiDAR to the real training data improves the mIoU by
2.2 and 2.6 percentage points on SemanticKITTI and SemanticPOSS,
respectively, and adding SynLiDAR translated towards the real domain improves
it by a further 2.2 and 2.6 percentage points (4.4 and 5.2 points above the
real-only baseline).

Consequently, downstream perception performance can be evaluated using
metrics appropriate to the perception task. For 3D object detection, average
precision is a principal metric. The KITTI benchmark, for example, evaluates
3D detection using precision-recall curves and average precision~\parencite{geigerAreWeReady2012a}. Recent work
on synthetic LiDAR similarly reports 3D AP, with some studies additionally
reporting bird's-eye-view and 2D detection performance~\parencite{marcusSynthItKITTI2026}. For semantic
segmentation, mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) is commonly used, as
demonstrated by SynLiDAR~\parencite{xiaoTransferLearningSynthetic2022}.

However, downstream perception metrics alone do not fully describe the
simulator's domain gap. A paired-scenario evaluation methodology~\parencite{manivasagamZeroDomainGap2023} reconstructs
a real-world scenario as a digital twin. LiDAR can then be simulated for the
same scenario and directly compared with the corresponding real LiDAR
measurement. This enables both sensor-level comparison and evaluation of the
effect of simulated sensor phenomena on the autonomy system.

An important finding of this paired-scenario work~\parencite{manivasagamZeroDomainGap2023} is that sensor-level realism and downstream
system performance are not necessarily equivalent. Phenomena such as motion
blur, material reflectance, multi-echo returns, and the geometric
reconstruction of traffic participants can affect the domain gap.
Furthermore, conventional perception metrics such as detection precision and
recall are not necessarily strong indicators of the domain gap with respect to
the final motion-planning task. Thus, the appropriate evaluation level depends
on the intended use of the synthetic dataset.

\subsection{Parameters important for synthetic data in general}
\label{sec:lidar-eval-general}

Several characteristics are relevant when evaluating synthetic data
independently of the sensing modality. 
The first is data diversity. A dataset must contain variation in the factors that influence the target task. In
autonomous driving, these factors can include scene layouts, object
configurations, environmental conditions, and traffic situations. Large
synthetic datasets such as SynLiDAR~\parencite{xiaoTransferLearningSynthetic2022} demonstrate the potential advantage of
synthetic environments in providing many annotated samples across diverse
environments. Diversity is important because in real circumstances, the autonomous system will encounter a wide range of situations, and a model trained on a narrow distribution of data may fail to generalise to unseen scenarios.

A second characteristic is distributional similarity between
synthetic and real data. Synthetic data can contain systematic differences
from the target domain, creating a domain gap. Such differences may concern
the distribution of objects, scenes, appearances, or sensor observations.
However, similarity alone should not be considered sufficient evidence of
dataset quality. A synthetic dataset can exhibit a small statistical
difference from real data while still failing to contain information that
improves the target perception task.

A third characteristic is annotation quality and availability. One
of the principal advantages of synthetic data is that ground-truth annotations
can be generated automatically together with the sensor data. SynLiDAR~\parencite{xiaoTransferLearningSynthetic2022}, for
example, provides point-wise annotations for 32 semantic classes and more
than 19 billion points. This allows large-scale training without the manual
annotation effort required for equivalent real-world point clouds.

Finally, the downstream utility of the synthetic data is a critical
criterion. This can be measured by comparing models trained under different
data regimes, for example:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item real data only;
    \item synthetic data only;
    \item synthetic data followed by real-data fine-tuning;
    \item real data augmented with synthetic data.
\end{enumerate}
The resulting models should then be evaluated on the same independent
real-world test set. The change in AP or mIoU relative to a real-only baseline
provides direct evidence of whether synthetic data contributes useful
information to the target task.

\subsection{Parameters specifically important for synthetic LiDAR data}
\label{sec:lidar-eval-lidar-specific}

In addition to general synthetic-data characteristics, LiDAR simulation
introduces sensor-specific requirements. A LiDAR simulator must reproduce not
only the geometry of the scene but also the characteristics of the sensing
process.

Point density and sparsity are important because LiDAR sensors produce
incomplete and non-uniform observations of the environment. The density of
measurements varies with distance, viewing angle, object geometry, and sensor
configuration. SynLiDAR explicitly identifies the synthetic-to-real gap as
containing a sparsity component and develops a point-cloud translation method
to address this difference~\parencite{xiaoTransferLearningSynthetic2022}.

Sensor noise and measurement artefacts are another important factor. LiDAR
measurements can contain noise, missing returns, spurious returns, and other
deviations from ideal geometric surfaces. A purely geometric ray-casting
simulation may therefore produce point clouds that are geometrically plausible
but sensor-wise unrealistic. LiDARsim addresses this by combining
physics-based ray casting with a learned model that generates deviations from
the idealised simulation~\parencite{manivasagamLiDARsimRealisticLiDAR2020}.

Intensity and material reflectance are also relevant. The response of LiDAR
measurements depends on the physical properties of surfaces. Material
modelling and reflectance are important factors affecting the domain gap~\parencite{manivasagamZeroDomainGap2023}.
Consequently, accurately reproducing object geometry alone does not guarantee
realistic LiDAR observations.

Temporal and scanning effects are particularly important for automotive
LiDAR. Since a point cloud is acquired over a period of time rather than
instantaneously, moving objects can produce motion distortion or motion blur.
Motion blur and other scanning effects can have an influence on the similarity
between simulated and real LiDAR and consequently on autonomous-system
behaviour~\parencite{manivasagamZeroDomainGap2023}.

Multi-echo and return phenomena can also contribute to the sensor domain gap.
Real LiDAR sensors can produce multiple returns from a single emitted pulse,
whereas simplified simulators may generate only a single idealised return. The
paired-scenario experiments~\parencite{manivasagamZeroDomainGap2023} demonstrate that such effects can influence the
realism of simulated point clouds.

Finally, scene and object geometry is important. Synthetic LiDAR depends
directly on the geometry presented to the simulated sensor. Inaccurate
representations of vehicles, pedestrians, or other traffic participants can
therefore create differences between simulated and real observations.
Accurate geometric reconstruction of traffic participants is one of the
important factors affecting the domain gap~\parencite{manivasagamZeroDomainGap2023}.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{sec:lidar-eval-conclusion}

The literature indicates that synthetic LiDAR should be evaluated at both the
data level and the downstream model level. Data-level
evaluation can assess characteristics such as point density, sparsity, sensor
noise, intensity, reflectance, geometric accuracy, and other LiDAR-specific
phenomena. These measurements are useful for determining whether a simulator
reproduces relevant properties of a real sensor.

However, the strongest evidence for the usefulness of a synthetic LiDAR
dataset is obtained through downstream evaluation on independent
real-world data. For 3D object detection, average precision (AP or mAP) is an
important primary metric, whereas semantic segmentation is commonly evaluated
using mIoU. The most informative experimental comparison is therefore not
simply whether synthetic and real point clouds are similar, but whether a
model trained using synthetic data maintains or improves its performance when
evaluated on real LiDAR data.

The literature suggests a hierarchy of evaluation.
Downstream real-world perception performance should form the main
criterion, while sensor realism and distributional similarity
provide supporting evidence explaining why a synthetic dataset
succeeds or fails. In particular, the sim-to-real performance gap and the
change in real-world AP or mIoU when synthetic data are introduced provide
direct evidence of the practical value of synthetic LiDAR. At the same time,
the paired-scenario work~\parencite{manivasagamZeroDomainGap2023} demonstrates that conventional perception metrics do
not necessarily capture all aspects of simulator quality, particularly when the
objective extends beyond perception to the behaviour of the complete
autonomous-driving system.

Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation of synthetic LiDAR should combine
(1) real-world downstream perception performance, (2) comparison
against real-data baselines, (3) data-level measures of sensor realism and
domain gap, and (4) evaluation under relevant variations and difficult
scenarios. This combination distinguishes a dataset that merely resembles
real LiDAR from one that provides useful information for training perception
models intended for real autonomous-driving environments.
