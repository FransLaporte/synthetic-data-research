\subsection{RQ1: General approaches to synthetic data generation}
\label{sec:rq1}
Generation methods differ in where they obtain structure: explicit
probability models, scene and physical rules, learned distributions, or
combinations of these. The categories overlap and do not imply a ranking
\parencite{nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019,hernandezSyntheticDataGeneration2022}.

\subsubsection{Statistical models and simulation}
Statistical methods sample fitted distributions and conditional
relationships. \Glspl{bayesian-network}, for example, represent dependencies
among variables. Their usefulness depends on whether those dependencies
retain the information needed for the target task
\parencite{hernandezSyntheticDataGeneration2022,hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023}.
Drawing more observations from a fitted model does not automatically
remove uncertainty in that model
\parencite{breugelSyntheticDataReal2023}. Tabular evidence illustrates this
principle but does not establish LiDAR geometry or sensor realism.

\Gls{procedural-generation} builds scenes through rules, assets and sampled
parameters; physical simulation models their observation. These stages
provide scenario control and access to scene labels, but their assumptions
limit what can be represented. \Gls{domain-randomization} varies parameters
within such a pipeline. It can expand the range of training conditions, yet
its sampling choices still determine whether that variation is relevant
\parencite{nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019}. Sensor effects absent from the
observation model remain absent even in a diverse scene collection.

\subsubsection{Learned neural generators}
A variational autoencoder (\gls{vae}) learns a probabilistic latent
representation and decoder. A generative adversarial network (\gls{gan})
learns through competition between a generator and discriminator. Both
depend on training examples, model capacity and learning objectives;
reconstruction quality or convincing individual samples alone does not
demonstrate coverage. Conditional models expose selected attributes as
controls, while mode collapse can restrict the variation a GAN produces
\parencite{hernandezSyntheticDataGeneration2022,hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023,
breugelSyntheticDataReal2023}.

Diffusion and score-based methods learn denoising behaviour or a
\gls{score-function}. LiDARGen provides a concrete example: it learns from
noisy range and intensity images and samples using repeated
\gls{langevin-dynamics} updates
\parencite{zyrianovLearningGenerateRealistic2022}.
Iterative sampling makes the update schedule part of the computation
budget. Other families, including \glspl{normalizing-flow}, also occur in
the broader literature \parencite{hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023}.
The selected studies do not justify transferring a tabular method ranking
to automotive LiDAR.

\subsubsection{Hybrids, augmentation and trade-offs}
Hybrid methods combine explicit structure with learned corrections.
LiDARsim reconstructs assets from real recordings, uses \gls{ray-casting}
to render them, and learns which returns to remove
\parencite{manivasagamLiDARsimRealisticLiDAR2020}.
This retains scene control while fitting sensor behaviour, at the cost of
requiring both assets and training data. Augmentation instead transforms
or recombines existing samples; the distinction depends on how much of the
scene and observation is reused
\parencite{nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019}.

In answer to RQ1, no family is universally preferable. The relevant
trade-offs are control, coverage, annotation support, real-data dependence
and computation (Table~\ref{tab:rq1-generation}). These choices must be
revisited at the sensor level before synthetic observations can be treated
as useful perception training data.
