\subsection{RQ1: General approaches to synthetic data generation}
\label{sec:rq1}
Synthetic data can be produced by specifying a model of a process, learning
a distribution from observations, or combining these approaches. This
section groups the selected literature into statistical models, procedural
and physical simulation, learned neural generators, and hybrid methods.
The groups describe how data are produced rather than which modality they
represent; they can overlap. For example, a simulator can sample parameters
from a learned distribution. This is a conceptual synthesis of selected
sources, not an exhaustive ranking of generators
\parencite{nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019,hernandezSyntheticDataGeneration2022}.

\subsubsection{Statistical models and conditional sampling}
Statistical approaches generate observations by sampling a fitted
probability model. The health-record review by
\textcite{hernandezSyntheticDataGeneration2022} describes models that
capture relationships between attributes and generate numerical or
categorical records. \Glspl{bayesian-network} are one example: variables and
their conditional dependencies define a joint distribution from which new
records can be sampled. They are included alongside neural generators in
the tabular benchmark of \textcite{hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023}.

The central assumption is that the chosen distribution and dependency
structure retain the relationships relevant to the intended task.
This gives an explicit place to encode modelling choices, but relationships
omitted or poorly estimated by the model need not reappear merely because
more samples are drawn. Accordingly, generating a larger dataset and
obtaining more information about the real population are different goals.
This distinction is consistent with the analysis of generative uncertainty
by \textcite{breugelSyntheticDataReal2023}. For this review, tabular studies
illustrate general modelling choices; they do not establish how well those
choices reproduce LiDAR geometry.

\subsubsection{Procedural generation and physical simulation}
\Gls{procedural-generation} constructs data through rules, assets and sampled
parameters. For a virtual scene, these can specify which objects appear
and how they are arranged. Physical simulation then models an observation
or interaction with that scene. These are complementary stages: constructing
a road layout is different from simulating a sensor's response to it.
Nikolenko's survey discusses both virtual environments and procedural
scene generation, including the difficulty of constructing plausible
relationships between objects rather than placing them independently
\parencite{nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019}.

Their attraction for supervised learning is control over the underlying
scene and access to scene information from which labels can be derived.
Their limitation is that the generated observations depend on the
completeness of the assets, rules and observation model. LiDARsim provides
a concrete example: its authors identify missing material and sensor effects
as sources of discrepancy between simulated and real scans
\parencite{manivasagamLiDARsimRealisticLiDAR2020}. Thus, control over a
scenario does not by itself establish realistic sensor measurements.

\Gls{domain-randomization} varies generation parameters to expose a trained
model to a wider range of conditions. In this review it is treated as a
strategy applied to a generator, rather than a separate generator family.
Nikolenko describes both broad randomization and approaches constrained
by scene structure \parencite{nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019}.
Our interpretation is that the sampling distribution is a substantive
design choice: increasing variation can expand coverage, but variation
must remain relevant to the deployment task. Detailed evidence about that
trade-off for LiDAR belongs in RQ2 and RQ3.

\subsubsection{Learned neural generators}
Neural generators learn a distribution or a sampling procedure from
training examples. Their appeal is that complicated relationships need
not all be specified by hand. However, their outputs still depend on the
training distribution, architecture and learning objective. The following
families illustrate different ways to formulate this learning problem.

\paragraph{Variational autoencoders.}
Autoencoder-based methods use an encoder to obtain a compact representation
and a decoder to reconstruct observations, as described by
\textcite{hernandezSyntheticDataGeneration2022}. A variational autoencoder
(\gls{vae}) is a probabilistic member of this family; Hansen et al.\ include its
tabular variant, \gls{tvae}, among the evaluated generators
\parencite{hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023}.
For the comparison here, the important distinction is that reconstructing
an input and producing useful new samples are different objectives.
The representation and the generation procedure must therefore be recorded
alongside downstream evidence, rather than treating an autoencoder's
reconstruction ability as sufficient evidence of synthetic-data utility.

\paragraph{Generative adversarial networks.}
A generative adversarial network (\gls{gan}) trains a generator against a
discriminator that distinguishes generated from training samples.
The generator learns to produce examples that challenge this discriminator
\parencite{hernandezSyntheticDataGeneration2022}.
Van Breugel et al.\ discuss \gls{mode-collapse} and poor coverage among the
limitations of learned synthetic data
\parencite{breugelSyntheticDataReal2023}.
This makes diversity a separate concern from the plausibility of individual
samples. A conditional generator can make selected attributes controllable,
as illustrated by tabular conditional GANs (\gls{ctgan}) in
\textcite{hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023}; the available conditioning
variables determine what is directly specified.

\paragraph{Diffusion and score-based models.}
Diffusion and score-based methods provide another route to learned
generation. Hansen et al.\ include a tabular \gls{diffusion-model} in their
benchmark \parencite{hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023}.
For a concrete sensor-data example, LiDARGen learns a \gls{score-function} from
\glslink{range-image}{range} and \gls{intensity} images perturbed at different noise levels. Generation
starts from noise and uses repeated updates through \gls{langevin-dynamics} \parencite{zyrianovLearningGenerateRealistic2022}.
This iterative sampling makes the number and schedule of updates practical
computational considerations. LiDARGen illustrates a score-based method;
its particular sampler should not be assumed to describe every diffusion
model or to establish a universal advantage over other generator families.

These are not the only neural approaches. \Glspl{normalizing-flow} use invertible
mappings between a reference distribution and observations and appear in
the tabular comparison by Hansen et al.
\parencite{hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023}.
The present synthesis concentrates on VAEs, GANs and denoising methods
because they provide useful connections to the selected LiDAR literature,
without asserting that the remaining families are unimportant.

\subsubsection{Hybrid methods and augmentation}
Hybrid pipelines combine explicit structure with learned components.
LiDARsim reconstructs scene assets from real recordings, performs \gls{ray-casting}, and learns which simulated rays should be dropped. It therefore
uses learning to model effects not adequately captured by the physical
stage \parencite{manivasagamLiDARsimRealisticLiDAR2020}.
This offers a way to retain scene control while fitting aspects of real
sensor behaviour. The corresponding dependency is twofold: the pipeline
needs both suitable scene assets and data for its learned component.
Hybrid generation consequently does not eliminate real-data requirements.

Figure~\ref{fig:lidarsim-object-reconstruction} illustrates the asset-building
stage: multiple observations are combined, incomplete geometry is filled
using vehicle symmetry, and the result is refined into a representation
made of \glspl{surfel}. These reconstructed assets can then be placed in
simulation scenes \parencite{manivasagamLiDARsimRealisticLiDAR2020}.

\Glslink{augmentation}{Augmentation} instead modifies available examples, for instance by
transforming an image or inserting an object into a scan. Nikolenko
distinguishes transformation-based augmentation from creating new data,
while LiDARGen discusses object insertion as a form of LiDAR simulation
\parencite{nikolenkoSyntheticDataDeep2019,zyrianovLearningGenerateRealistic2022}.
The boundary is therefore partly task-dependent. This review records what
is newly generated and what is reused, rather than assigning all
augmentation methods to a separate, mutually exclusive family.

The entries describe dependencies to investigate, not measured rankings.
In particular, synthetic sample count is not a substitute for evidence of
utility. Hansen et al.\ distinguish statistical fidelity from several forms
of downstream usefulness, and van Breugel et al.\ demonstrate that testing
on synthetic data can misrepresent performance on real data in their
studied settings
\parencite{hansenReimaginingSyntheticTabular2023,breugelSyntheticDataReal2023}.

In answer to RQ1, the main approaches differ in where their assumptions
reside: explicit probability models, scene and physical rules, learned
distributions, or combinations of these. Their practical trade-offs concern
control, coverage, annotation support, dependence on real data and
computational resources. The selected evidence does not support choosing
one family as universally best for machine learning. For the LiDAR-focused
review, this motivates comparing both how scenes are constructed and how
sensor observations are generated. RQ2 examines those choices in detail;
RQ3 examines whether the resulting data supports perception on real scans.
