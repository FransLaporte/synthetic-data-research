\section{Problem statement and relevance}\label{sec:problem}
As outlined in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, data-level diagnostics such as FRD, BEV MMD and BEV JSD are commonly used as a less costly alternative to training a model and evaluating it on real data~\cite{zyrianovLearningGenerateRealistic2022}. These diagnostics summarise the distribution of features or point densities over complete sets of scans and do not involve any perception task, which raises the question of how well they reflect downstream performance.

The available evidence indicates that these diagnostics are an incomplete indicator of downstream performance. In the comparison of generative LiDAR models by Zyrianov et al.~\cite{zyrianovLearningGenerateRealistic2022}, the diagnostics do not rank the methods in the same order, so the conclusion depends on which diagnostic is chosen. Manivasagam et al.~\cite{manivasagamZeroDomainGap2023} similarly report that aggregate detection metrics do not necessarily reflect outcomes at the level of the complete autonomy system. Real driving scans are strongly imbalanced, as classes such as road, sidewalk, building, vegetation and terrain are the most frequent, whereas classes such as motorcyclist occur only rarely and objects such as bicycles and poles are represented by few points in a single scan~\cite{behleySemanticKITTIDatasetSemantic2019}. Our literature review therefore argued that a synthetic dataset can closely match this frequent content while misrepresenting rare, small or distant objects. Since such objects make up only a small share of the points in a scan, errors in their representation may barely affect a diagnostic computed over complete scans. This is problematic, because these objects are precisely the cases that synthetic data is often intended to supplement.

It is therefore plausible that the diagnostics detect large deviations between synthetic and real data but miss smaller deviations that already reduce downstream performance. To our knowledge, however, the sensitivity of the diagnostics relative to downstream performance has not been quantified. In particular, it is unknown how far a sensor characteristic such as ray drop can deviate before the diagnostics register a change, and how this level compares with the degradation at which the class- and range-specific performance of a trained model begins to decline.

This gap has practical consequences for researchers and developers who rely on synthetic LiDAR data. If the diagnostics react later than downstream performance, a favourable score may lead to the adoption of a synthetic dataset that impairs the perception of vulnerable road users or distant objects, a failure that directly affects the safety of autonomous vehicles. If the diagnostics react earlier, useful datasets may be rejected without need. The problem addressed in this proposal is therefore that the reliability of data-level diagnostics as a substitute for real-data evaluation is unknown, so that it cannot be decided when costly real-data evaluation is actually required and when it can be omitted.
