\section{Hypotheses}\label{sec:hypotheses}
This section states an expectation for each sub-question, and together these expectations form an anticipated answer to the main question. To make the comparison between the diagnostics and downstream performance measurable, the hypotheses are expressed in terms of two reaction points, which mark where each side first responds on the degradation scale. The degradation level $d$ is the expected fraction of points removed by ray drop, tested at 5, 10, 20, 30 and 40\,\%, so that a lower reaction point means that a measure already responds when fewer points have been removed. The performance drop point is the lowest degradation level at which a performance measure falls noticeably below its value for the baseline dataset ($d=0$, Section~\ref{sec:data}). The diagnostic reaction point is the lowest level at which a diagnostic differs from its baseline value by more than its normal variation. Both points are defined precisely in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}, which also describes how reaction points that are not reached within the tested range are treated. Each hypothesis also states the outcome that would refute it.

\textbf{H1 (SQ1).} FRD reacts to both forms of ray drop, whereas BEV MMD and BEV JSD only react to uniform ray drop after more points have been removed than for range-dependent ray drop, or do not react to it at all within the tested range. As described in Section~\ref{sec:scope}, uniform ray drop removes every point with the same probability, whereas range-dependent ray drop removes distant points with a higher probability than nearby points. In the LiDARGen implementation, both BEV diagnostics are computed on histograms that are normalised to sum to one, so that each cell holds the fraction of all points of a scan that falls within it~\cite{zyrianovLearningGenerateRealistic2022}. Uniform ray drop removes, in expectation, the same fraction of points from every cell, which leaves these fractions unchanged and only makes the histograms noisier as fewer points remain. Range-dependent ray drop removes relatively more points from distant cells and therefore shifts the fractions towards cells close to the sensor. FRD is instead computed from RangeNet++ features of range images, in which every removed point appears as an empty pixel regardless of its position. Both forms of ray drop thus change the features extracted from the synthetic scans, and FRD is expected to increase under both. H1 is refuted if the BEV diagnostics react to uniform ray drop at the same or a lower degradation level than to range-dependent ray drop, that is, after the same or a smaller fraction of points has been removed, or if FRD does not react to one of the two forms within the tested range. Levels are compared on the tested grid, as described in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}.

\textbf{H2 (SQ2).} The IoU of small-object classes and the mIoU of the far-range band decline at a lower degradation level than the overall mIoU. The small-object classes are fixed in advance as bicycle, motorcycle, person, bicyclist, motorcyclist, pole and traffic sign, insofar as the class mapping retains them, and the far-range band contains the points beyond 40\,m (Section~\ref{sec:model}). These objects are represented by few points, and the point density of a LiDAR scan decreases with distance. A model trained on degraded data therefore observes them with even fewer points, which makes their shape harder to learn, while large and nearby surfaces such as road and buildings remain well represented. This effect is expected to be stronger for range-dependent ray drop, since that form removes distant points with a higher probability. H2 consists of two parts, which are assessed separately for each form of ray drop. The class part is supported if most small-object classes reach their drop point at a lower level than the overall mIoU and refuted if most reach it at the same or a higher level, while any other outcome is reported as mixed. The range part is refuted if the far-range band reaches its drop point at the same or a higher level than the overall mIoU.

\textbf{H3 (SQ3 and main question).} Each diagnostic reacts at a higher degradation level than the overall mIoU. The diagnostics summarise complete scans, which are dominated by frequent classes and nearby points. A decline that is concentrated in small and distant objects, as expected under H2, therefore affects only a small share of the points that determine the diagnostic score. This expectation is consistent with the evidence discussed in Section~\ref{sec:problem}, which suggests that these diagnostics are an incomplete indicator of downstream performance~\cite{zyrianovLearningGenerateRealistic2022,manivasagamZeroDomainGap2023}. H3 is assessed for each combination of diagnostic and form of ray drop, and is refuted for a combination if the diagnostic reacts at the same or a lower level than the overall mIoU. Because H2 predicts that small and distant objects decline even before the overall mIoU, the lag is expected to be largest for these cases, but this follows from H2 and is not tested as a separate prediction.

Together, the hypotheses describe an expected pattern in which the diagnostics react later than downstream performance, most strongly for uniform ray drop and for the small and distant objects discussed in Section~\ref{sec:problem}. If this pattern is confirmed, a favourable diagnostic score would be insufficient grounds for omitting real-data evaluation when this type of degradation is subtle, since a clear change in a diagnostic would only appear after performance had already declined. The experiment covers a single dataset, model and degradation family, however, so turning this observation into a general rule for rejecting unseen datasets without training a model would require further validation. If instead the diagnostics react in step with the mIoU, this would support their use as a less costly screening step under the tested conditions. The method described in Section~\ref{sec:method} is designed to distinguish between these outcomes.
